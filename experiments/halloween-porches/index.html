The porch that ate Halloween.

One porch advertises eight candies. It can serve one goblin. The sign-rewarded crowd moves in anyway. Reward the whole neighborhood, and the bags fill—but somebody may spend every Halloween at the two-candy house.

The problem isn't always dishonesty.

Each goblin visits exactly one porch. The advertised candy number is real. Capacity is real too. Overcrowded porches use a fair lottery: one winner receives the whole listed amount, while the others leave empty-handed.

When individuals seek their own candy, some stable distributions leave useful capacity unused. When a group maximizes all candy, it can split across porches perfectly. But total productivity and fair individual experiences are different measurements.

What survives when the rules change?

  • Try abundant supplies: the ten goblins can all collect eight candies at one porch. Concentration now produces 80 candies; a detector that always penalizes crowding would be wrong.
  • Try personal reward: best-response switches can stop at 35 or 38 even though 43 is attainable. A stable choice is not necessarily an efficient outcome.
  • Try shared reward: the total aligns with the 43-candy target, while the fixed-role payoff distribution remains unequal.
  • Try rotating identities: expected fairness can improve without reducing output here, but this toy does not model travel, specialization costs, preferences, communication, or real agents.
