Where do the goblins go shopping?

Twenty-four goblin shoppers receive the same six-store directory. Some follow the crowd, some chase a deal, and some insist on checking the record. Change the promotion, the browsing budget, and the order they arrive. Watch which storefronts attract attention—and where the eventual carts go.

Known market generator

The six entirely fictional stores

Quality and value scores (1–10) are fabricated and held fixed across the two arms. The only promotion changes Golden Goblin Goods' listing appeal; it does not change quality, price/value or dossier evidence. An evidence-oriented shopper hard-excludes a shop only after opening credible ongoing-harm documentation in this made-up world.

Three observations worth separating: the directory delivered six listing cards; an explicit open is the action-level attention proxy; a selection is the scripted cart decision. None reveals an agent's private motive. Shortlists are recorded and are not the same as final purchases. In fact, no money or purchasing tool exists here.

Research lineage · What the demo can and cannot say

The unexpected bit isn't where they click. It's where they don't buy.

New research note: The Shop That Sold Nothing. In the seed-72, two-open demonstration, the promoted seller gets 11 visits and 0 carts, yet 16 shoppers change which other shop they select. Five never open the promoted seller at all. This is a selected post-hoc scripted case, not a real-agent result.

Under the seeded default, promoting Golden Goblin Goods reroutes 14 of 24 browsing paths. That merchant moves from zero to thirteen recorded opens, yet remains at zero final selections. Some carts change owners, but others don't. More exposure didn't translate into a proportional change in choice.

Turn the browse budget down to one and the same promotion can change final selections. Turn it up to six—so every shop is inspected—and the promotion can change visit order but no longer changes final selections in this particular fixed-product model. Neither outcome establishes how real shoppers, language models, or web traffic behave.

It can even make attention less concentrated.

Under the default social-feedback run, the neutral-world opening concentration (Herfindahl index) is 0.2550. The promoted world's value is 0.2199. Why? The baseline already draws the crowd to other strong stores; introducing another attractive listing spreads the visits. The word promotion doesn't logically entail higher concentration.

Feedback can change paths

With prior click counts shown, moving a shopper earlier or later can redirect what they open. Turn feedback off; reversing arrival order leaves each shopper's scripted choices unchanged. The difference is observable in the recorded trace, not an assertion about hidden intention.

The homogeneous control is boring—and useful

Replace six mixed shopper profiles with 24 reliability-seekers. The promotion has little or no effect. The toy does not force every treatment to matter. Shopper heterogeneity was part of the generator; it was not independently discovered.

What we ran before any outsider environment

We implemented and executed 2,048 paired scripted scenarios: 128 deterministic seeds, four browsing budgets, two feedback settings and two profile rosters. Each has two arms of 24 policy shoppers: 98,304 scripted policy executions, not 98,304 language-model calls. Ten Python regression tests passed; the browser JavaScript implementation was also cross-checked against the Python-generated finite sweep. All counts are generated inside a known toy world and describe that generator.

This is a separate demonstration descendant of our October 21 Who Gets the Cart? pilot. That older test bed had 216 synthetic worlds and 56 passing engineering tests, but zero independent model-agent episodes. Its 48-episode starter schedule remains unrun. We have not run an outsider model environment, made a real purchase, browsed real sellers, authenticated provider identities, or learned a shopping agent's internal attention.

Related work includes WebShop, AgenticShop, and Turpin et al. on explanation faithfulness. These motivate the need to instrument shopping decisions; none validate our toy's outcomes.
