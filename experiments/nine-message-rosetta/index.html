A difficult question asked of two neural civilizations

Nine messages.
4,096 meanings.

Two independently trained goblin tribes invented incompatible private vocabularies. We gave a translator just nine paired utterances—no dictionary, no model weights, and no meanings—and challenged it to translate every possible combination.

The scary failure

A tribe can swap two already known symbols after calibration. Both words remain plausible; the translator silently gets it wrong. We did not solve drift detection.

Why the ninth question matters

Methods, receipts, and what this isn't

What was actually executed

Each independent tribe was trained from random weights with a straight-through discrete message channel and cross-entropy objective. It saw 512 sampled meanings of 4,096. Each tribe's channel has four positions, twelve possible glyphs per position, and a randomly chosen mapping of features to positions. None shared weights or learned words.

Our separate interpreter enumerated all 4! = 24 ways to match source and destination positions and inferred injective glyph maps from the paired utterances. It was given only nine examples, then locked before the other 4,087 oracle outputs were revealed.

10 independent pairs, 20 saved neural checkpoints, 7 named verification checks. The complete source package includes frozen results, model weights, hard-mode attempts, a replay verifier and the local protocol. No pretrained model, network call, AI API or external data access was used in training.

Why we'd try this

Current research on emergent communication, generational learning, convention formation and model collectives asks when agents can transmit structured information without sharing a human vocabulary. Our demo isolates one tractable case of that problem—and one failure mode.

Context: Botoko Ekila et al., TACL 2026 (decentralized conventions in continuous spaces); Dziewoński et al., CoNLL 2026 (turnover and drift); Brinkmann et al., Nature Communications 2026 (human transmission of machine-discovered strategies). These papers do not validate this local test.

The open problem: can we get the same nine-message decoding leverage when the agents are free to invent their own unstructured protocols—and detect drift that reuses familiar symbols? We have not accomplished either.

Status: bounded, same-author, locally reproduced. No live LLM agents, no independent validation, no adoption or deployment, and no claim that artificial goblins model human cultures.
